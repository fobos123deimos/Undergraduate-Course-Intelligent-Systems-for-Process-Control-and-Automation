## Problem 09.1 - Predict sensor regimes and interpret a stationary distribution

(a) The next regime depends on the present regime rather than earlier history, and the same transition probabilities apply at every sample. Matrix multiplication gives $P^2=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0.83&0.17\\0.34&0.66\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Hence the probability of drift at 0.4 min is 0.17; this includes both normal-to-normal-to-drift and normal-to-drift-to-drift paths. (b) Balance requires $0.1\pi_N=0.2\pi_D$, with $\pi_N+\pi_D=1$, so $\pi=[2/3,1/3]$. Writing $x_n=\Pr(z_n=N)$ gives $x_{n+1}=0.2+0.7x_n$, hence $p_n=[2/3+(1/3)0.7^n,(1/3)(1-0.7^n)]$. Thus $p_5=[0.72269,0.27731]$ at 1 min. Positive entries ensure irreducibility and aperiodicity. (c) A normal episode survives each transition with probability 0.9. Its duration, including the initial normal sample, has mean $1/(1-0.9)=10$ sample intervals, or 2 min. (d) The stationary vector describes stable occupancy proportions, while individual paths continue switching. For $P=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, $[1/2,1/2]$ is stationary but $[1,0]P^n$ alternates forever; aperiodicity is missing.

## Problem 09.2 - Separate expected lifetime from near-term fault probability

(a) The transient block is $Q=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0.8&0.15\\0.1&0.7\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. First-step conditioning gives $t_N=1+0.8t_N+0.15t_D$ and $t_D=1+0.1t_N+0.7t_D$. Solving $0.2t_N-0.15t_D=1$ and $-0.1t_N+0.3t_D=1$ yields $t_N=10$ and $t_D=20/3$ steps. Equivalently, the row sums of $(I-Q)^{-1}$ give these values. (b) Failure by the second transition includes immediate failure, staying normal then failing, and degrading then failing: $0.05+0.8(0.05)+0.15(0.2)=0.12$. The complementary probability of remaining transient is 0.88. (c) Expected times are 2 min from normal and $4/3$ min from degraded. Early failure is possible, so neither is a guaranteed run time. (d) The laboratory chain allows fault recovery and has no absorbing fault state. A first-arrival question can be posed by stopping that chain on first entry into fault, but the corresponding transient block must be derived from its own matrix. If some initial state can remain in a closed healthy class forever, absorption need not occur almost surely; an unconditional expected time can be infinite and the finite inverse formula may fail.

## Problem 09.3 - Check three different HMM inference questions by enumeration

(a) The initial unnormalized vector is $[0.30,0.04]$, giving scale $s_0=0.34$ and filtered distribution $[0.882352941,0.117647059]$. After prediction, emission and normalization, the remaining scales are $s_1=0.634117647$ and $s_2=0.322894249$. The filtered distributions are $[0.524118738,0.475881262]$ and $[0.862876350,0.137123650]$. Their scale product is $\Pr(o)=0.069616$; its natural logarithm is $-2.664760853$. (b) In lexicographic path order $000,001,010,011,100,101,110,111$, the joint probabilities with the observed record are $0.03675$, $0.00315$, $0.01620$, $0.00486$, $0.00280$, $0.00024$, $0.00432$ and $0.001296$. They sum to 0.069616. Sum the paths with state 0 at a chosen sample and divide by this total: smoothed state-0 probabilities are $0.875660768$, $0.616812227$ and $0.862876350$. The final value matches filtering because no later observations remain. (c) The largest joint probability is 0.03675 for path 000. Viterbi maximizes one whole path; the sequence likelihood sums all paths. Although the marginal decisions happen to agree with Viterbi here, this is not a general identity. (d) At the second sample the available filtered distribution is $[0.524118738,0.475881262]$. The second smoothed value uses the third observation and cannot support a causal decision at that earlier time; the full-record Viterbi path also uses later data. Products of many subunit probabilities underflow, so retain normalized filtering vectors with the sum of log scales, and use log probabilities for Viterbi.

## Problem 09.4 - Learn from separate trajectories and preserve state meaning

(a) Initial counts are $[1,1]$, giving $\hat p_0=[1/2,1/2]$ after smoothing. Within-sequence transition counts are $C=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, giving $\hat P=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}3/5&2/5\\1/2&1/2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Emission counts are $E=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}3&1\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, so $\hat B=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2/3&1/3\\1/5&4/5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Emissions include each final sample, while transitions require a following sample in the same experiment. (b) Concatenation fabricates a transition from state 1 at the end of the first experiment to state 1 at the start of the second. The second transition row becomes $[2/5,3/5]$ after pseudocounts, instead of $[1/2,1/2]$. Treating the combined record as one sequence would also discard one initial-state observation. (c) Forward-backward supplies expected occupancies $\gamma_k(i)$ and adjacent-state probabilities $\xi_k(i,j)$. Sum $\xi$ only over valid within-trajectory pairs for transitions; sum $\gamma$ at each observed symbol for emissions; average the initial occupancies across experiments for the initial distribution. Repeat inference and normalized expected-count updates. (d) Exact unregularized EM improves or preserves its training objective, subject to numerical tolerance; it need not find the global maximum or improve held-out detection. A permutation of hidden labels leaves the observation likelihood unchanged, so semantic fault labels require independent justification. Reserve complete experiments with different state/noise seeds and operating schedules; choose bins, state count and alarm threshold using training/validation only. Evaluate the frozen model on a final separate record, reporting likelihood and state-aware diagnostic metrics when independent labels exist.

## Problem 09.5 - Turn a posterior into a reviewable supervisory decision

(a) The expected bias is $0.10(0)+0.25(0.055)+0.65(0.14)=0.10475$, so the corrected measurement is 0.59525. Prediction gives next-step fault probability $0.10(0.005)+0.25(0.03)+0.65(0.90)=0.593$. This prediction precedes the next observation; it is not that future sample's filtered probability. (b) The fixed 0.7 rule produces no alarm because 0.65 is below threshold. In the separate one-sample cost model, expected alarm cost is $2(1-p)$ and no-alarm cost is $8p$, giving alarm when $p>2/(2+8)=0.2$ (either action ties at 0.2). Thus the cost model selects an alarm at $p=0.65$. A 0.7 policy encodes a different tradeoff; delay, repeated alarms and action costs require a sequential policy model rather than silently reusing the single-sample result. (c) Use the same plant, initial state, reference, hidden regimes, noise and actuator limits. Each controller receives only measurements and past applied inputs; the corrected loop uses the current filtered posterior, never true labels, smoothing or full-record Viterbi. Freeze parameters and thresholds before test evaluation. Report tracking error, input effort, false alarms, missed faults, episode detection delays and calibration alongside the probability trajectories. (d) A slower actual tank creates a systematic model residual during transients. The HMM can interpret this plant mismatch as sensor bias and subtract a nonexistent defect, potentially worsening control. Stress-test nominal and mismatched plants separately, retain the raw-measurement baseline and document failures. Independent instrument data, validated fault labels, model adequacy, timing and a separate control/protection assessment are needed; successful synthetic classification supplies no formal stability or safety guarantee.
